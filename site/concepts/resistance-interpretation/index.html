    Resistance interpretation¶

    Every other part of Ligase is about keeping data straight. This is the one part that says what the data means: it takes variant calls against HXB2, works out which amino acids each codon of protease, reverse transcriptase, integrase and capsid holds, and scores them against the Stanford HIVdb algorithm.

    @lg.step(tool="hivdb")
def resistance(
    v: Variants, ref: Reference, aln: Alignment
) -> ResistanceReport:
    return lg.hivdb.interpret(v, ref, coverage=aln)

    The output is one row per drug: a score, a level from Susceptible to High-Level Resistance, the mutations that got it there, and the algorithm that decided.

    The rules are vendored, not fetched¶

    Stanford publishes the HIVdb algorithm as ASI2 XML in its own repository, hivdb/hivfacts, under the Unlicense — a dedication to the public domain. Ligase ships two versions of it, pinned by checksum: 9.8, which is what the published results it is validated against were produced with, and 10.2, which is current.

    Stanford also runs a web service that does this interpretation. Ligase does not call it, and the reason is the same one behind every other decision here: an answer that depends on the day it was asked for is not reproducible. No cache key can see a remote service change its mind, and no reproducibility bundle can carry it. docs/decisions.md D8 puts public-data fetching out of scope for v0.1 in any case.

    So the rules are part of the engine, and which rules were used is part of the cache key — by the digest of their bytes rather than by their version number. Upstream publishes corrections under near-identical names (HIVDB_10.0 and HIVDB_10.0-1 both exist), and a key that moved only with the name would happily serve a result computed under rules that have since changed.

    Four judgements, all made the same way¶

    An interpretation can be wrong in two directions, and they are not equally bad. Reporting resistance that is not there costs a patient a drug they could have taken. Reporting susceptibility that is not there costs them a drug that will fail. Everywhere these two could be traded off, Ligase takes the first.

    A mixture is every amino acid it could be¶

    Where the reads support both the reference base and an alternate above the mixture threshold, the codon holds both amino acids, and a rule is satisfied if any of them satisfies it.

    An ambiguous position is two viruses, not an average of one. Taking the majority base would discard the minority virus — which is the entire reason anyone sequences deeply, and which in practice is where resistance shows up first.

    A codon is only the combinations the reads carry¶

    A mixture at two bases of one codon is where the first judgement could go wrong on its own. A VCF says what is at each position, not what travels together, so enumerating the combinations of two ambiguous bases invents codons no read carries.

    On Sample 3 of PRJNA1218827 the caller credits G at the first base of RT codon 219 in 43% of the reads there and at the second base in 2.4%. At a 2% threshold both pass, and the four combinations spell lysine, arginine, glutamate and glycine — where the reads spanning the whole codon carry two of them: AAA in 54% of themselves and GAA in 45%. The other two are under two tenths of a per cent each.

    So the alignment settles it. Reads spanning all three bases are counted as the triplets they carry, and the codon holds the amino acids whose share of those reads reaches the mixture threshold. Two rules keep this from going wrong in the other direction:

    • Phasing narrows a reading and never widens one. What is present at a position stays the caller's judgement, which weighs base quality; the reads only decide which combinations of called alleles are real.
    • A codon no read spans keeps its per-base reading, noted rather than dropped or guessed at — see D31, which also says how far that note is visible today.

    A position that was not read is not a wild-type position¶

    bcftools writes only the sites it has something to say about. A position missing from a VCF is therefore ambiguous between "agrees with the reference" and "nothing aligned here" — and those have opposite clinical meanings.

    So coverage is read from the alignment. A codon whose bases are not all covered to the required depth is unread, not reference, and a drug whose rules mention an unread position reports its score as a floor rather than as an answer, naming the positions it could not see.

    Without an alignment nothing can be known about coverage, and the reading says so in a note rather than assuming quietly. That assumption has a direction — it manufactures susceptible results out of dropouts — and the one thing it must never be is silent. The report itself cannot say it, since a report with nothing known to be unread looks exactly like one that was fully covered, so the step hands the note to the engine and the engine says it: in the run report, in provenance, in a bundle and in the methods section (D32).

    The hedges a reading makes with an alignment travel the same way. A gene no codon of which reached the depth asked for, a gene only partly read, and codons no read spans all three bases of are each the difference between a score and a floor, and each is said rather than left for someone to infer from an unread column.

    The test that pins this is a no-template control. Sixty of its thirty-two thousand reads map to HXB2. A pipeline that read "no variant called" as "agrees with the reference" would report a tube of water as fully susceptible to all twenty-seven drugs.

    A gene nobody sequenced is not assessed¶

    HIVDB 9.8 scores lenacapavir against capsid, which is in gag. A pipeline that sequenced only pol has nothing whatever to say about it — so the report says "Not assessed" rather than "Susceptible".

    This is D24's reasoning about citations applied to a drug class: a gap that is named is honest, and a gap that is filled with a cheerful default is not.

    What it is checked against¶

    The scoring engine reproduces 3,348 published Stanford HIVdb results exactly — thirty clinical samples at four ambiguity thresholds, every drug, every score, every level. Those expectations come from Sierra 3.5.3, a different implementation in a different language by the algorithm's own authors, so agreeing with them is evidence rather than circularity.

    The pipeline end to end is checked in validation.
