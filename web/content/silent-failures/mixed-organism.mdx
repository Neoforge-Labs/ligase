# My pathogen results include host reads

<Lede>
You sequenced a patient sample and called variants against the virus. Some of
them are in places the virus is not variable, or are supported by a handful of
reads, or look like human sequence once you go and look. The pipeline ran
cleanly, the VCF is valid, and nothing said the sample was **more than one
organism**.
</Lede>

## What actually happened

A host–pathogen sample is not one organism. A blood or sputum sample from a
person with HIV-1 contains the person's DNA and RNA as well as the virus's. A
metagenome contains many organisms. A xenograft contains two. The reads in the
file are a mixture, and the alignment step has to decide where each one goes.

Reads from the host that resemble the viral reference, because of a repeat, a
low-complexity stretch, or an endogenous element, can map to it anyway. They
arrive at the variant caller looking like reads from the virus that differ from
the reference. The caller reports the difference as a variant, and a later step
interprets that variant as viral variation, including variation in genes that
matter for drug resistance.

The usual defence is a decontamination step, such as aligning to the host first
and removing what maps there. That is a step somebody has to remember to add.

## Why nothing crashed

Before this check existed, a dataset in Ligase could declare **one** organism.
Somebody with a host–pathogen alignment had to pick, and in practice they picked
the pathogen, because that is what the analysis is about. The reference was the
virus, and so the declaration `organism="HIV-1"` on the alignment agreed with the
declaration `organism="HIV-1"` on the reference. The two sides said the same
thing and the comparison passed.

Everything below that comparison was also fine. The alignment's `@SQ` header
named the viral contigs, with the lengths and checksums the reference has. The
BAM was valid. Each individual fact was true; what was missing was a place to
say that the alignment was about two organisms, and nothing could object to a
statement that was never made.

An ordinary tool has the same blind spot for a plain reason. A variant caller
sees reads and a reference. It has no concept of what organism the reads came
from, so it cannot tell host reads from viral reads, and it has no business
guessing.

## What Ligase says

The `organism` dimension is a **set**. The reference here says it is about HIV-1
and *Homo sapiens*, as a host–pathogen reference is. The alignment was declared
as the pathogen alone, which is how it would have been written before.

<Diagnostic note="ligase check — nothing ran">{diagnostics.mixedOrganism}</Diagnostic>

Three things are worth noticing.

The two declarations are shown **side by side, each against the name it is bound
to**: `aln.organism` first, then `ref.organism`. Each is marked `(declared)`,
because no alignment header and no FASTA records an organism, so these are
statements that a person made. Nothing was inferred.

The line after the two declarations names **the difference itself**:
`ref also lists Homo sapiens`.
You are not left comparing two lists by eye to find the extra member. For a
metagenome with a dozen organisms on one side and eleven on the other, that line
is the part you read.

And the closing question is worded for the general case. For this
particular message "Did you pass data from the wrong organism?" is a little
beside the point, since nobody passed the wrong data. The accurate reading is
that the two sides disagree about what they are about. Either the alignment is
a host–pathogen sample that was declared as only its pathogen, or the reference
was declared too broadly. The message tells you which sets differ and leaves the
choice of fix to the person who knows the sample.

## How it is caught

Pass a list wherever the data is about more than one organism:

```python
import ligase as lg

ref = lg.reference(
    "data/refs/hxb2_plus_host.fasta",
    organism=["HIV-1", "Homo sapiens"],
)
aln = lg.alignment(
    "data/alignments/sample01.bam",
    organism=["HIV-1", "Homo sapiens"],
)

# Nothing has run. This reads the FASTA index and the BAM header.
lg.check_step("call", {"ref": ref, "aln": aln})
```

Two organism sets are equal when they name the same organisms, in any order, so
`["Homo sapiens", "HIV-1"]` and `["HIV-1", "Homo sapiens"]` agree. A single
string is a set of one.

<Note kind="warning" title="This checks what was declared, not what is in the reads">
Ligase does not read sequence content for this, and it cannot tell you that a
BAM contains host reads. It does not screen for contamination. What the
dimension does is make each side state what it is about, and refuse to connect
two sides whose statements disagree. A sample that really is host–pathogen,
declared as such on the data and on the reference, passes the check. Whether a
decontamination step is needed, and whether it worked, is still a question about
the reads.

If you declare a host–pathogen sample as its pathogen alone *and* use a
pathogen-only reference, the declarations agree and the check has nothing to
object to, just as before. The check can only be as informative as the
declarations you give it.
</Note>
