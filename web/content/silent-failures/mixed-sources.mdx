# My target ranking changed and I cannot find the analysis change

<Lede>
You rerun last quarter's target prioritisation and the top twenty is not the
top twenty. Nothing in your code changed, the algorithm is the same algorithm,
and the scores are all computed correctly. So you go looking for the parameter
you nudged — and there is none, because what changed is that the graph you
ran it on was rebuilt, and the rebuild folded in one more evidence source.
</Lede>

## What actually happened

A knowledge graph is not measured. It is **assembled**: somebody took Open
Targets, a literature-mined association set, a pathway database and an
in-house curation file, and merged them into nodes and edges. What the graph
holds is a function of that list.

Add one source and edges appear. Edges appearing is not a cosmetic change: a
gene that had three neighbours now has five, so every quantity computed from
the neighbourhood moves. Degree moves. Any propagation or diffusion over the
graph moves, and moves further than the new edges, because a score that
spreads along edges spreads along the new ones too. A rank is a comparison
between scores, so a rank that did not move numerically still moves
positionally when the gene below it did.

Drop a source and the same thing happens in reverse, with a worse reading
attached: an association that is absent from the graph looks like an
association the evidence does not support.

## Why nothing in the file says so

Look at a node. It has an id, a type, perhaps some attributes. Look at an
edge: a source, a target, a type, maybe a score. Each of those is a fact
about one piece of the graph. Which sources went into the **graph** is a fact
about the whole of it, and there is nowhere in a node or an edge for it to
live.

Graphs that carry per-edge provenance are better off — the edge says it came
from DisGeNET — and still do not answer the question, because the list of
sources that contributed is the union over every edge, and nothing computes
that union and writes it down where a comparison would see it. Two graphs
sitting next to each other on disk disclose their difference only to someone
who reads all of both.

## Why the answer is plausible rather than absurd

Because it is arithmetically correct. The scores are right for the graph they
were computed on. The ranking is the right ranking of those scores. Every
intermediate value would survive any audit you could run on the computation,
because the computation is not where the problem is.

And the magnitude is in the plausible range. A new source does not usually
rewrite a ranking; it moves things by a few places and swaps two or three
entries near the cut. That is precisely the size of effect people attribute to
a methodological choice, a parameter, a different random seed, or a genuine
improvement — which is why the usual response to it is to write it up.

## Where it bites hardest

**A benchmark.** You evaluate a method against a graph built in March and a
colleague evaluates theirs against one built in September. The two numbers are
compared in a table as though they measured two methods. They measured two
graphs.

**A comparison across diseases.** Two graphs assembled with different source
coverage, because the curation effort differed by therapeutic area, are
compared for which disease has the better-connected target landscape. The
answer is about the curators.

**A reproduction.** Someone reruns your pipeline a year later, against today's
assembly, and gets a different answer. Nothing is wrong with either run, and
there is no artefact left over to tell them that.

## What Ligase says

The sources a graph was assembled from are a dimension — `source_set` — and
the loader reads it off the graph's own nodes and edges rather than asking
you for it. Two graphs that disagree on it do not join:

<Diagnostic note="ligase check — nothing ran">{diagnostics.graphSources}</Diagnostic>

Three things are worth noticing.

Neither side was declared. Both say `from the sources named on its nodes and
edges`, naming the file: the engine read the per-edge provenance and took the
union, so you can open the file it names and check.

Both source sets are printed in full, and then the member that makes them
differ is named on its own line: `theirs also lists disgenet.` For two graphs
with a dozen sources each, that line is the only part worth reading.

And the closing question is the one a user can act on — *was a source added or
removed upstream?* — rather than a restatement that the two sets differ. It
points at the rebuild, which is where the change was.

## How it is caught

Load each graph. Nothing is declared about the sources, because the graphs
say:

```python
import ligase as lg

march = lg.knowledge_graph("graphs/2026-03.json", organism="Homo sapiens")
september = lg.knowledge_graph("graphs/2026-09.json", organism="Homo sapiens")

# Nothing has run. This read both files' nodes and edges.
lg.check_step("compare", {"ours": march, "theirs": september})
```

That refuses with the message above. There is no flag that makes it pass,
because the two graphs really are different graphs, and the fix is yours to
choose: reassemble both from the same source list, or stop comparing them and
say in the write-up that they are not comparable.

What the engine will do is carry the dimension forward. A step over a graph
inherits its `source_set`, so a ranking computed from the September graph
carries September's sources, and anything that tries to score it against
March's is refused the same way — three steps later, on a file that no longer
mentions a source anywhere.

<Note kind="caution" title="A source is compared exactly, case included">
`s3://evidence/A.json` and `s3://evidence/a.json` are **two** sources, and two
graphs naming one each do not join. That is deliberate: an object key and a
URL path are case-sensitive by specification, and folding the case would
quietly merge two different objects into one — turning two graphs that differ
into two that agree, which is the failure this page is about.

The cost is that `GSE12345` and `gse12345` are also two sources, where a
person would read them as one study. That costs you one consistent spelling.
The other way round costs a dropped member and no message, which is worse. An
`organism` does fold case, because `HIV-1` and `hiv-1` are one organism by
convention — see
[D36](/project/decisions/#d36--release-is-its-own-dimension-reference-keeps-its-meaning).
</Note>

<Note kind="note" title="A graph that names no sources is not a graph with none">
If neither the graph nor any of its nodes and edges names a source, the
dimension is **unknown**, with a reason — not an empty set. Two graphs that
each named none would otherwise agree on the axis, which is exactly the
silence the dimension exists to break. Declare `source_set=[...]` on a graph
you assembled by hand.
</Note>
