# The number in my paper is not the number my pipeline produces

<Lede>
A reviewer asks where a number came from. You go back to the pipeline, and
the pipeline says something else. Not because anything is broken: the
analysis ran correctly, the output is correct, and the sentence was correct
when it was written. Somewhere between the first draft and submission the two
stopped describing each other, and nothing in your tools was watching.
</Lede>

## What actually happened

One of four things, and each is an ordinary afternoon's work:

- **A threshold moved.** The draft said *above 1% allele frequency* because
  that is what `min_freq` was in March. It is 5% now. Nothing failed; the
  sentence simply stopped being true.
- **A sample was dropped.** The results table says 24. One failed QC and was
  removed from the manifest, so the pipeline runs 23 and the table does not
  know.
- **A tool was upgraded.** The methods section names `bwa 0.7.17`. The
  cluster has 0.7.18 and nobody announced it.
- **A step was renamed or removed.** The reported count came from an analysis
  that has since been rewritten, so the number in the paper now corresponds
  to no step at all.

## Why nothing catches it

Because the number and the pipeline live in different worlds, and no tool
spans both. A workflow engine knows what ran; it has never read your
manuscript. A reference manager knows your citations; it has never seen your
parameters. Version control sees both files change and has no idea that one
change invalidates a sentence in the other.

Even careful provenance stops one step short. Ligase was already unusually
good at naming what it could not vouch for — it names a tool with no citation
rather than omitting it
([D24](/project/decisions/#d24--a-citation-is-curated-or-declared-never-guessed)),
names a sample identifier that is really a file name
([D26](/project/decisions/#d26--a-field-that-cannot-be-dropped-is-noticed-instead)),
names a step it cannot account for. All of that runs in one direction: from a
result back to how it was produced. The question a reader of the paper asks
runs the other way, and there was nothing to ask it of.

## Why the answer is plausible rather than absurd

Because nothing is wrong except the sentence. Rerun the pipeline and it
succeeds. Check the output and it is correct. Audit the step and it
reproduces. Every verification you can run passes, because the computation is
not where the problem is — and the number in the paper is in the plausible
range, because it was produced by an earlier version of the same analysis.

That is what makes this the most expensive kind of mistake in the catalogue.
It survives peer review, because a reviewer cannot see your pipeline. It
survives a reproducibility check, because the pipeline reproduces. It is
found, if it is found at all, by somebody trying to build on the paper.

## What Ligase does about it

You tell it the number, and the step behind it:

```python
pipe = lg.Pipeline(
    call(hxb2, min_freq=0.05),
    claims=[
        lg.Claim(
            "variants were called above 1% allele frequency",
            step="call",
            parameter="min_freq",
            value=0.01,
        ),
    ],
)
```

<RunReport>{claims.contradicted}</RunReport>

Both numbers are stated and `ligase methods` exits non-zero, so the build
that moved the threshold is the build that tells you the manuscript needs
editing. The write-up does not guess which of the two should change, because
it cannot know whether the paper or the pipeline is the thing that is behind.

For the renamed step, the same mechanism says the other thing:

<RunReport>{claims.unbacked}</RunReport>

## Or point it at the number itself

The claim above compares a parameter the run **recorded**. Point a claim at
the number in the **output** and the engine reads it:

```python
lg.Claim(
    "malaria F1 = 0.333 against seven curated targets",
    step="validate",
    value=0.333,
    at=lg.cell(column="f1", row=0),
    tolerance=5e-4,
)
```

<RunReport>{claims.verified}</RunReport>

Rerun the analysis and leave the manuscript alone, and the verdict turns
over:

<RunReport>{claims.refuted}</RunReport>

This is the form that catches the failure at the top of this page directly:
the table in the paper and the number in the artifact have diverged, and the
build says so rather than passing
([D61](/project/decisions/#d61--a-claim-may-name-where-its-number-lives)).

## What it does not claim to do

It does not go looking for a number it was not pointed at, and it parses one
format: a TSV with a header, a column by name, a row by position. A claim
that cannot say where its number is binds to the step key, the output digest
and the date that computation happened — enough for a reviewer with your
bundle to find the computation and rerun it, and not a verification of the
number. That is a legitimate state rather than a shortcoming, and
[the guide](/guide/claims/) says which claims are in it.

It is also not a statistics engine. It does not recompute your F1 or check
that it is a well-formed F1. The promise is narrower and worth more: the
number you published is the number in the bytes.
