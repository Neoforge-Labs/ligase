# My compound overlap is almost empty

<Lede>
You score your hit list against the vendor library it was screened from, and
the overlap is forty compounds out of two thousand. Not zero — zero would
have told you something. Forty. So you conclude the assay was noisy, or the
screen enriched for something the library under-represents, and you write
that down. What actually happened is that one of the two files had its salts
stripped and the other did not.
</Lede>

## What actually happened

An InChIKey is a hash of a molecular structure. Change the structure and the
key changes, and "standardisation" is a set of operations that change the
structure on purpose:

- **Salt stripping** removes counterions and solvents, so aspirin sodium
  becomes aspirin.
- **Charge neutralisation** removes formal charges where a neutral form
  exists, so a deprotonated carboxylate becomes a carboxylic acid.
- **Tautomer normalisation** picks one tautomer per tautomeric form, so
  warfarin's keto and enol forms become one compound.

Every one of those is correct, standard, recommended practice. None of them
is recorded in the file that comes out. So a column of InChIKeys is the
answer to a question — *which substances are these, and in what state?* — of
which the file states only the first half.

## How big the difference actually is

Measured, across thirty ordinary drugs: **twelve of them get a different
InChIKey under a different standardisation state.** Forty per cent, and the
thirty were marketed drugs, salts and tautomer pairs, not curiosities.

Aspirin, arriving as its sodium salt the way a vendor ships it, has three
keys:

| What was done to it | InChIKey |
|---|---|
| nothing | `JZLOKWGVGHYBKD-UHFFFAOYSA-M` |
| salts stripped | `BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-M` |
| salts stripped, charges neutralised | `BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-N` |

Read the last two again. They differ in the **final character** — the charge
block — and the third is the key plain aspirin gets. So a library that had its
salts stripped and a library that had its salts stripped *and* was neutralised
do not match on aspirin, and nothing about either column of keys suggests why.

Warfarin is the other shape of the same problem. Its keto and enol forms are
two keys raw and one key after tautomer normalisation, so a normalised set and
an unnormalised set overlap in **nothing** for warfarin.

## Why the answer is plausible rather than absurd

Because the unaffected compounds still match. Eighteen of those thirty are
the same key either way, so an overlap of two libraries that differ in
standardisation is not empty: it is roughly the fraction of compounds that
happen to have no salt, no charge and no tautomer. That is a number in the
tens of per cent, which is exactly the range people attribute to assay noise,
library composition, a different cut-off, or a real biological finding.

A zero would have been a gift. A plausible number gets written up.

## Why no glance at the data catches it

This is the part that makes it worse than [the gene-set version of the same
failure](/silent-failures/mixed-identifiers/). Two gene lists in different
identifier spaces *look* different: `TP53` is visibly not
`ENSG00000141510`, and anyone eyeballing two columns sees it in a second.

Two compound sets standardised differently look identical. Both are columns of
twenty-seven-character strings in the same format, with the same shape, from
the same software. `BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-M` and
`BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-N` are one character apart, in the block nobody
reads, and no amount of looking at the file will tell you which standardiser
produced it.

## Where it bites hardest

**A virtual screen scored against its own library.** The hit list was
standardised by the docking pipeline; the library was downloaded. The
enrichment you compute is partly a measurement of your standardiser.

**Merging two vendor catalogues.** The same substance arrives twice under two
keys, so a "unique compounds" count is wrong, and anything that averages over
compounds double-counts the ones that were duplicated.

**A benchmark against a public set.** ChEMBL standardises its compounds and
says so; a vendor file does not. Comparing your hits to ChEMBL measures the
difference between your preparation and theirs, and comes out plausible.

**Stereochemistry, which is the one with a clinical edge** and has a page of
its own, because its symptom is the opposite of this one's: RDKit's tautomer
canonicaliser discards stereochemistry, so two enantiomers become one key and
an overlap comes back too **large** rather than too small. See [my inactive
enantiomer came back as a hit](/silent-failures/discarded-stereo/).

## What Ligase says instead

A compound table carries a line saying what standardised it, and the engine
reads it:

<Diagnostic note="ligase check — nothing ran">{diagnostics.molecularStandardisation}</Diagnostic>

Both states are printed in full, the operations that make them differ are
named on their own line, and the closing question is the one to act on — *were
the two sets standardised differently?* — rather than a restatement that they
are not equal.

Where the two differ by more than one operation, that line is the whole of
what is worth reading:

<Diagnostic note="ligase check — nothing ran">{diagnostics.molecularTautomers}</Diagnostic>

And two tables that each say **nothing** are refused rather than allowed to
agree by silence, because agreeing by silence is this failure in its purest
form:

<Diagnostic note="ligase check — nothing ran">{diagnostics.molecularUndeclared}</Diagnostic>

## The version of this that two honest declarations produce

The worst shape of it is not an undeclared file. It is two files that are
both telling the truth.

`salt-stripped` does not pin a charge state and an InChIKey's final block
does. So a reference set that was salt-stripped and left anionic, and one
that was salt-stripped and then neutralised, can both carry the honest
declaration `inchikey; salt-stripped` — the values compare equal, the join
goes ahead, and the same generated set scores 0% novel against one of them
and 50% against the other. A consumer measured exactly that, with `ligase
check` reporting `no problems found` and exiting 0 for both.

The vocabulary was coarser than the identifier it keys to, which is the same
defect as an inferred value that compares equal to another inferred value,
one level down. Two things changed:

- **Charge state is now checked against the keys.** A table declared
  `charge-neutralised` whose keys end in anything but `N` is refused at load,
  because that is a claim the file refutes.
- **`salt-stripped` alone is documented as the trap it is.** Public
  collections ship neutral parents, so the one-term form produces keys that
  match nothing in the wild. [Comparing compound
  sets](/guide/molecules/#the-trap-worth-knowing-before-you-choose-a-state)
  says so where someone is choosing a state.

What is still not checked is the part no key records: salt stripping and
tautomer normalisation leave no trace, so those remain recorded on your word
([D65](/project/decisions/#d65--a-declaration-is-refused-where-the-file-itself-refutes-it)).

## How it is caught

```python
import ligase as lg

library = lg.molecule_set("vendor/catalogue.tsv")
hits = lg.molecule_set("screen/hits.tsv")

# Nothing has run. This read both files' representation lines.
lg.check_step("overlap", {"library": library, "hits": hits})
```

The fix is yours to choose, and the engine does not choose it for you:
standardise both to the same state, or stop comparing them and say in the
write-up that they are not comparable. What it will not do is let the
comparison through and let you read the result as chemistry.

Where you do standardise, `lg.chem.standardize` writes the state it arrived
in onto its own output, so the next step is checked against a true statement
rather than a claim nobody verified. See [comparing compound
sets](/guide/molecules/).

<Note kind="caution" title="Salt stripping alone is not enough">
`FragmentParent` leaves the deprotonated carboxylate behind, so a
salt-stripped set still does not match a neutral one. Two files that both say
"salts removed" can still fail to overlap if only one of them was also
neutralised — which is the trap in the aspirin table above, and the reason
`representation` records the operations as a **set** rather than as a single
level of cleanliness.
</Note>
