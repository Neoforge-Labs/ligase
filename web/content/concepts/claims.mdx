# A number in a paper

Provenance answers one question: *how was this result produced?* A claim asks
the other one, and until now Ligase could not answer it: *what is behind the
number I published?*

## The asymmetry this closes

`ligase methods` is unusually careful about what it does not know. It names a
tool it has no reference for rather than leaving it out of the reference
list, because "a tool silently missing from a reference list lets an
incomplete methods section look complete"
([D24](/project/decisions/#d24--a-citation-is-curated-or-declared-never-guessed)).
It names a sample identifier that is really a file name
([D26](/project/decisions/#d26--a-field-that-cannot-be-dropped-is-noticed-instead)).
It names a step whose production it cannot account for, rather than printing
a paragraph with a silent hole in it.

Three different promises, and all the same one: **the record names what it
cannot account for.**

Now look at what it was silent about. The paper says *variants were called
above 1% allele frequency*. The pipeline says `min_freq = 0.05`, because the
threshold moved in June and the sentence did not. Both statements are in
front of you, in the same terminal, and nothing connects them. The engine
will tell you that `fastp` has no citation and say nothing at all about the
number in your abstract — because nothing has ever told it what that number
is.

A claim is how it is told.

## What a claim is

A sentence a paper states, and the step that produced it:

```python
pipe = lg.Pipeline(
    samples.map(lambda s: resistance(call(align(s, hxb2), hxb2))),
    claims=[
        lg.Claim(
            "variants were called above 1% allele frequency",
            step="call",
            parameter="min_freq",
            value=0.01,
        ),
    ],
)
```

The sentence is yours and is never checked. Everything else is: that `call`
is a step this pipeline produces, that `min_freq` is a parameter it takes,
and that the run held it at `0.01`. What comes back is the digest of what the
step produced, and the date that computation happened:

<RunReport>{claims.bound}</RunReport>

## Why the sentence and the value are two fields

A paper writes **1%**. The pipeline holds **0.01**. Comparing the sentence
against the parameter would be a guess about arithmetic in English, and a
mechanism built on that guess would be wrong in both directions — calling
correct papers wrong and, worse, occasionally calling a wrong one right.

So they are separate and each does one job. `text` is what you grep the
write-up for with the manuscript open beside it. `value` is what the engine
compares, written the way the pipeline holds it.

## The three verdicts

**Bound.** Every check passed. The number rests on a computation this
write-up accounts for, named by key.

**Contradicted.** Something stands behind the number and disagrees with it.
This is the drift above, caught:

<RunReport>{claims.contradicted}</RunReport>

Both numbers are stated, so the fix needs no investigation — and the write-up
does not guess which of the two is wrong, because it cannot know whether the
paper or the pipeline is the thing that should move.

**Unbacked.** Nothing in the run stands behind it at all:

<RunReport>{claims.unbacked}</RunReport>

There are five ways to land here and the complaint distinguishes them,
because a renamed step, a dropped sample, a renamed parameter and a swapped
tool are four different one-line fixes. A claim whose step is a
[gap](/guide/provenance/) is the fifth: the step exists, its record does not,
and a claim resting on a step nothing is known about rests on nothing.

## Why a declaration, and not a guess

The engine does not open your VCF and count its rows. It could do that for
the formats it knows and would have to skip the rest, which is the
lenient-canonicaliser failure in a new costume: a mechanism that verified
some numbers and silently waved the others through.

What it does instead is narrower and honest. A claim with a `value` is
*compared*. A claim without one is *bound* — to a digest, a key and a date, so
a reviewer with the bundle can find the computation and rerun it. Neither is
"the engine checked your arithmetic", and the pages do not say it is.

The one thing a claim must never be is a sentence reprinted under a heading.
M13 put it in one line: **a declaration nothing can check is a comment.** A
`Claims` section that merely echoed your prose would make an unchecked
paragraph look checked, which is the exact failure D24 refuses in the
reference list — so every part of a claim except its text is checked against
the run, and a claim that cannot survive that is named rather than printed.

## What a claim is bound to

The **computation**, never the occasion. A claim binds to the step key, and a
key is already the identity of a computation: the same code, the same
parameters, the same inputs, the same tool versions. So a step served from
the cache backs its claim completely, and the date you see is when that
computation happened — which may be months before the run that wrote the
paper up.

This is deliberate and it is [D45](/project/decisions/#d45--a-claim-binds-to-the-computation-not-to-the-run-that-fetched-it).
The alternative would make `ligase methods --force` the only honest way to
write up a paper, which is the opposite of what a content-addressed cache is
for.

What a claim is bound to and what it is **cited by** are then two different
things, and conflating them cost a consumer a test. The key is the identity
of a computation, so it moves when the computation's code, parameters,
inputs or tool versions move — and `cite=` moves the key while the artifact
stays byte-identical and nothing reruns. The **output digest** is what
survives such an edit, and it is what `audit` and a bundle verify against,
so the digest is the identifier a write-up prints and a paper quotes
([D59](/project/decisions/#d59--a-claim-is-cited-by-its-output-digest-not-by-its-step-key)).

The same decision has a second half: **a claim is in no cache key.** Editing
a sentence in your manuscript reruns nothing. A mechanism whose cost was a
re-alignment per draft would simply not be kept up to date, which is the
worst available outcome for a feature whose entire job is to be current.
